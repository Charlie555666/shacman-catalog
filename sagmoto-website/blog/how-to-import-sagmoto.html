How to Import SAGMOTO Trucks: Complete Guide for International Fleet Buyers

August 4, 2026Guide8 min read

Importing commercial trucks from China is a process that, while straightforward when handled by an experienced exporter, involves multiple stages that require careful planning. From selecting the right model configuration to navigating customs clearance in your destination country, each step carries decisions that affect your total cost, delivery timeline, and long-term satisfaction with your investment. This guide walks through the entire import process for SAGMOTO trucks, based on the real-world export experience of Shaanxi Fenghan Trading Co., Ltd., an authorized SAGMOTO exporter shipping to Africa, the Middle East, CIS countries, and Southeast Asia.

Step 1: Choose Your Model and Configuration

The process begins with matching a truck model to your operational requirements. SAGMOTO produces trucks across four primary categories — light duty (3.5–6 ton GVW), medium duty (8–16 ton GVW), heavy duty (31–49 ton GCW), and off-road vocational trucks. Within each category, you must specify the engine output, transmission type, wheelbase, axle configuration, cab style, and optional equipment.

Key configuration decisions include:

  • Engine selection: SAGMOTO offers engines from 180 HP to 560 HP. Long-haul operators typically need 460 HP or above; regional distribution can use 290–420 HP; light urban delivery requires 150–180 HP.
  • Left-hand vs. right-hand drive: SAGMOTO produces both LHD and RHD configurations. Specify your country's road-side requirement early — it affects production lead time.
  • Emission standard: Euro III, Euro IV, Euro V, and Euro VI are available. Your destination country's emission regulation determines which standard you can register. Many African and CIS markets accept Euro III/IV; Middle Eastern Gulf countries increasingly require Euro V.
  • Cab configuration: Day cab, sleeper cab, or high-roof sleeper. Long-haul operators should select the sleeper or high-roof sleeper; urban distribution typically uses day cabs.
  • Axle ratio: Affects top speed vs. gradeability. Highway operations favor faster ratios (2.64–3.08); mixed terrain needs 3.7–4.11; heavy vocational work requires 5.26 or lower.
  • Body type: Tractor head, flatbed, box body, tipper, mixer, or fuel tanker. Specify the body type and whether SAGMOTO should supply it factory-fitted or if you will fit the body locally.
Pro tip: Provide your exporter with your typical route profile (distance, terrain, payload weight, climate) and let their engineering team recommend the optimal configuration. Fenghan Trading's technical team offers this consultation at no cost.

Step 2: Get a Proforma Invoice and Pay Deposit

Once your configuration is finalized, the exporter issues a Proforma Invoice (PI). This document specifies the truck model, configuration details, quantity, unit price, total price, Incoterms (FOB, CIF, or DAP — discussed below), payment terms, estimated production lead time, and validity period of the offer.

Standard payment terms for SAGMOTO truck exports are:

  • 30% T/T (telegraphic transfer) deposit upon order confirmation, which triggers factory production scheduling.
  • 70% balance payment before shipment, due after the trucks pass pre-shipment inspection and before the Bill of Lading is issued.

For larger fleet orders (typically 10+ units), a Letter of Credit (L/C) is an alternative payment method. An irrevocable L/C at sight provides security for both buyer and seller — the buyer's bank guarantees payment upon presentation of shipping documents, and the seller ships knowing funds are secured. L/C processing adds approximately 5-7 business days to the timeline and incurs bank fees of 0.1-0.25% of the L/C value.

Step 3: Factory Production and Inspection (2–4 Weeks)

After the deposit is received, the factory schedules production. For standard configurations, the production cycle is approximately 2-3 weeks. For customized configurations — special axle ratios, unique cab colors, right-hand drive, or non-standard body types — expect 3-4 weeks.

Before shipment, a pre-shipment inspection is conducted. This is a critical quality gate that verifies:

  • Engine and chassis numbers match the Proforma Invoice specifications
  • All configured options and accessories are present and functional
  • Fluid levels (engine oil, coolant, brake fluid, AdBlue where applicable) are correct
  • Tire pressure, wheel torque, and suspension components are to spec
  • Electrical systems — lights, indicators, instrument panel, warning buzzers — are operational
  • Body panels, paint quality, and cab interior are free of defects
  • Emission compliance documentation and conformity certificates are included

Buyers or their appointed third-party inspection agents (such as SGS, Bureau Veritas, or TÜV) may attend the inspection. Fenghan Trading provides a comprehensive inspection report with photographs and video documentation for every shipment, regardless of whether a third-party inspector is present.

Step 4: Shipping Methods — RoRo vs. Container

Two primary shipping methods are used for truck exports from China: Roll-on/Roll-off (RoRo) and container shipping. Each has distinct cost, timing, and logistical considerations.

RoRo (Roll-on/Roll-off)

RoRo vessels are designed specifically for rolling stock. Trucks are driven onto the vessel at the port of departure and driven off at the destination port. RoRo is generally the preferred method for complete, assembled trucks because it is faster to load and unload, involves less handling damage risk, and does not require disassembly.

RoRo is available from major Chinese ports including Shanghai, Tianjin, Qingdao, and Guangzhou. The main limitation is that RoRo service is only available to ports with RoRo terminals — some smaller destination ports require transshipment, which adds 7-10 days.

Container Shipping

For destinations without RoRo service, or for smaller trucks that fit within container dimensions, 40-foot HQ containers are used. A standard heavy-duty tractor truck does not fit in a 40ft container without partial disassembly — typically the side mirrors, exhaust stack, and front bumper are removed and the cab is tilted slightly. Two light-duty trucks can sometimes share a 40ft container.

Container shipping is slower (loading and unassembly take 2-3 days each end) and involves more handling, but it serves ports that RoRo vessels cannot reach and allows consolidation of parts and accessories in the same container.

Step 5: Customs Clearance and Documentation

Upon arrival at the destination port, the truck must clear customs before it can be collected. The following documents are required for customs clearance in virtually all destination countries:

  • Bill of Lading (B/L): The transport document issued by the shipping line, serving as title to the goods. The consignee name on the B/L determines who can collect the truck.
  • Commercial Invoice: Shows the transaction value, payment terms, and Incoterms. Customs authorities use this to assess import duty.
  • Packing List: Details the contents, weight, dimensions, and packaging. For single-vehicle shipments, this also serves as the cargo manifest.
  • Certificate of Origin (COO): Issued by the China Council for the Promotion of International Trade (CCPIT). This document proves the goods were manufactured in China and is essential for preferential tariff treatment under free trade agreements (e.g., China-ASEAN FTA, RCEP).
  • Vehicle Conformity Certificate / Emission Certificate: Confirms the truck meets the emission standard declared on the Commercial Invoice. Required for registration in regulated markets.
  • Insurance Policy: Cargo insurance covering the voyage. If you purchased CIF terms, the exporter provides this. Under FOB, you arrange your own.

Your destination customs broker (a licensed agent who handles customs formalities on your behalf) will submit these documents, pay the applicable import duties and taxes on your behalf, and arrange for the truck's release from the port.

Step 6: Homologation and Local Registration

Homologation is the process of certifying that a vehicle meets your country's technical and safety regulations before it can be registered for road use. The requirements vary significantly by country:

  • West African countries: Many ECOWAS member states accept an E-mark (ECE) certificate or a BV (Bureau Veritas) inspection certificate. No in-country testing is typically required.
  • East African Community (EAC): Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, and Burundi accept vehicles with ECE certificates. A pre-export verification of conformity (PVOC) is required before shipment.
  • Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC): Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Oman, and Bahrain require a GCC certificate of conformity. The exporter applies for this through an accredited testing body before shipment.
  • CIS countries: Russia requires OTTS (Vehicle Type Approval); Belarus, Kazakhstan, and Armenia require EAC certification under the Customs Union. These require factory documentation and, for some categories, sample vehicle testing.
  • Southeast Asia: Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam, and Malaysia each have their own type-approval systems. SAGMOTO trucks are homologated in several of these markets; check current status with your exporter.

After homologation, the truck is registered at your local vehicle licensing authority. This typically requires the customs clearance certificate, the homologation certificate, proof of insurance, and the original purchase documents. Registration plates and a vehicle logbook are issued, completing the legal importation.

Understanding Incoterms: FOB, CIF, and DAP

The Incoterm you select defines who is responsible for shipping, insurance, and risk at each stage of the journey:

  • FOB (Free On Board): The exporter delivers the truck, cleared for export, onto the vessel at the Chinese port. From that point, the buyer assumes all costs and risks — ocean freight, insurance, destination port charges, customs clearance, and inland delivery. FOB gives the buyer maximum control and often the lowest total cost if they have a good freight forwarder.
  • CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight): The exporter covers the cost of the truck, ocean freight, and minimum marine insurance to the destination port. The buyer handles destination customs clearance, duties, and inland delivery. CIF is simpler for buyers who want the exporter to handle the shipping leg.
  • DAP (Delivered At Place): The exporter is responsible for delivering the truck to a named destination — typically your warehouse or operating base — including all transport and customs clearance in the destination country. The buyer only pays import duties and taxes. DAP is the most convenient but carries the highest cost because the exporter charges a margin for managing destination logistics.

For most first-time importers, CIF offers the best balance of simplicity and cost. As you gain experience and establish relationships with freight forwarders, FOB often becomes more economical.

Import Duties by Region: What to Budget

Import duties are a major component of your total landed cost and vary widely by region:

  • Africa: Duties range from 10% (Egypt) to 35% (Nigeria). Many African countries offer reduced or zero duty for commercial vehicles under specific HS codes. East African Community applies a 25% common external tariff on heavy trucks.
  • Middle East: GCC countries apply a standard 5% import duty on commercial vehicles. Some countries exempt trucks used for public transport or logistics infrastructure projects.
  • CIS: Russia applies 15% duty plus 20% VAT. EAEU members (Belarus, Kazakhstan, Armenia, Kyrgyzstan) follow similar structures. Free trade agreements with Vietnam and Iran may reduce duties.
  • Southeast Asia: Under the China-ASEAN Free Trade Area, most truck HS codes carry 0-5% duty with a valid Certificate of Origin. Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia all benefit from this. The Philippines applies 5-10%.
  • South America: Duties are high — typically 20-35% — and some countries apply additional local content requirements or non-tariff barriers.

Always confirm the current HS code classification and duty rate with your destination customs broker before placing an order. Duty rates change, and preferential trade agreements are continuously updated.
