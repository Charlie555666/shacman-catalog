SAGMOTO Z3 vs E1st Tractor Truck: Which Long-Haul Flagship Fits Your Fleet?

August 10, 2026Comparison Guide9 min read

Fleet managers shopping for a SAGMOTO tractor truck face a defining question: should you go with the Z3 conventional tractor, a proven workhorse engineered for fuel-efficient cross-border logistics, or step up to the E1st flagship, a 560HP premium long-haul machine built around the Cummins Z14? Both trucks share the SAGMOTO DNA — Cummins power, automated manual transmissions, and flat-floor cabs with 2.13 meters of standing height — but they are tuned for fundamentally different operational philosophies. This comparison breaks down the engineering, economics, and real-world trade-offs so you can make the right call for your routes, your cargo, and your balance sheet.

Powertrain: Cummins M13 vs Cummins Z14

The most consequential difference between these two tractors sits under the hood. The Z3 is powered by the Cummins M13, a 13-liter inline-six turbodiesel available in two calibrations: 480 HP at 2,200 Nm for standard 40-ton GCW operations, and 520 HP at 2,400 Nm for heavier 49-ton loads or mountainous terrain. The M13 carries a B10 life rating of 1.5 million kilometers, meaning 90% of engines reach that mileage before major overhaul — a figure that puts it in the same durability bracket as engines costing 30% more.

The E1st, by contrast, runs the Cummins Z14 — a 13.48-liter inline-six that represents Cummins' premium highway platform. It delivers 560 HP (412 kW) at 1,700 rpm and a peak torque of 2,650 Nm across an exceptionally wide plateau from 1,000 to 1,400 rpm. That extra 40-80 horsepower and 250-450 Nm of torque may seem marginal on paper, but in practice it transforms the driving experience on sustained grades. Where the M13 in the Z3 may require a downshift on a 6% incline with a full 49-ton load, the Z14 in the E1st holds its gear and maintains momentum — reducing drivetrain stress, driver fatigue, and trip time.

Key Insight: The Z14's torque plateau spans 1,000-1,400 rpm — one of the widest in the segment. This means the E1st rarely needs downshifts on rolling grades, keeping the engine in its most efficient operating range. For fleets running routes with significant elevation changes, this alone can deliver 2-3% better fuel economy than a truck with a narrower torque band.

Transmission and Driveline

Both trucks use automated manual transmissions, but they come from different suppliers with distinct shift logic. The Z3 is equipped with a 12-speed AMT developed jointly with the M13 engine's ECU — the shift strategy adapts to gradient, vehicle mass, and driver behavior over time. The E1st pairs its Z14 with an Eaton AMT (12 forward + 2 reverse gears), which uses GPS topographic data for predictive shifting. The Eaton system pre-selects gears before cresting hills, avoiding the fuel-wasting hesitation that plagues reactive AMT systems.

For fleets that prefer manual control, the Z3 offers a 12-speed manual variant. The E1st is AMT-only, reflecting its positioning as a premium, driver-friendly flagship. In practice, the AMT adoption rate in export markets exceeds 85% for both models — drivers are increasingly hard to find, and automated shifting removes the single largest source of fuel-economy variability between drivers.

Fuel Economy: Where the Numbers Matter Most

Fuel is the largest single line item in any fleet's operating budget — typically 35-45% of total cost of ownership for long-haul tractors. The E1st achieves 26.97 L/100km in fully loaded highway conditions at 49-ton GCW, a figure validated through cumulative fleet data. The Z3, with its smaller-displacement M13 engine and available 4x2 configuration, delivers approximately 25.5-27 L/100km depending on axle ratio selection and route profile.

The Z3's fuel advantage comes from two sources. First, the 4x2 configuration has a lower tare weight — roughly 400-500 kg less than a comparable 6x4 — which directly reduces fuel consumption. Second, the available 3.083 axle ratio (versus the E1st's 2.643) lowers engine RPM at cruise speed by about 8%, translating to an estimated 2-3 L/100km saving on highway-dominated routes. However, this comes at the cost of gradeability — the taller ratio means the Z3 will downshift more frequently on steep grades, partially eroding the fuel advantage on mountainous routes.

The E1st counters with superior aerodynamics. Its CFD-optimized cab exterior — including a roof deflector, side fairings, and chassis side skirts — achieves a drag coefficient of 0.45, among the best in the heavy-duty segment. At highway speeds above 80 km/h, aerodynamic drag accounts for up to 40% of total resistance, so the E1st's aero advantage narrows the fuel-economy gap considerably on high-speed corridors.

Cab Comfort: Shared DNA, Different Ambitions

Both cabs share the 2.13-meter flat-floor design that eliminates the engine tunnel hump, creating an uninterrupted walk-through from the driver's seat to the sleeper berth. Both feature high-roof configurations, overhead storage compartments, and climate control with independent diesel-fired heating for overnight stops. The fundamental cab architecture is similar — the difference lies in the details.

The E1st adds several premium touches that matter on multi-day hauls: heated and ventilated seats with pneumatic lumbar support, a multi-function steering wheel with integrated engine brake and cruise controls, and a more refined interior trim package. The Z3's cab is functional and comfortable — the 780mm wide lower bunk with high-density foam, the 350-liter overhead storage, and the roof-mounted parking air conditioner are all standard — but it lacks the E1st's attention to long-haul driver ergonomics.

Driver Retention Factor: In markets where experienced long-haul drivers are scarce — Central Asia, the Middle East, and East Africa — cab comfort is not a luxury, it is a recruitment tool. Fleets running the E1st report 15-20% lower driver turnover compared to conventional-cab alternatives. For operations where driver continuity directly affects fuel economy and cargo safety, the E1st's premium cab pays for itself.

Configuration Flexibility

This is where the Z3 pulls ahead decisively. The Z3 is available in both 4x2 and 6x4 configurations, making it adaptable to a wider range of operations. The 4x2 Z3 is the fleet manager's choice for container terminal shuttle routes and regional distribution — its lighter tare weight maximizes payload within the 40-ton GCW limit, and a single drive axle reduces tire costs. The 6x4 Long Range variant, with its 1,000-liter dual-tank setup and 3.083 final drive, is purpose-built for transcontinental corridors like the China-Central Asia route where fuel stops are measured in countries rather than kilometers.

The E1st is offered exclusively as a 6x4 — it is engineered for heavy 49-ton GCW operations where the extra drive axle's traction and braking capacity are non-negotiable. Its 800-liter dual fuel tanks provide substantial range, but fall short of the Z3 6x4 Long Range's 1,000-liter capacity. For routes where refueling infrastructure is sparse, the Z3 remains the more practical choice.

Decision Framework: Which Truck Should You Buy?

Choose the Z3 if your operation matches this profile:

  • Route type: Predominantly flat highway corridors, container terminal shuttles, or regional distribution under 500 km per trip.
  • Cargo weight: Standard 40-ton GCW operations where a 4x2 configuration is legal and practical.
  • Route distance: Cross-border corridors exceeding 2,000 km where the 1,000-liter fuel capacity eliminates refueling stops (Z3 6x4 Long Range).
  • Budget priority: Minimizing upfront purchase price and per-kilometer operating cost is the primary objective.
  • Driver market: Experienced drivers are available, and cab premium features are not a retention lever.

Choose the E1st if your operation matches this profile:

  • Route type: Mountainous corridors with sustained grades, or high-speed toll highways where aerodynamic efficiency matters above 80 km/h.
  • Cargo weight: Heavy 49-ton GCW operations requiring maximum power and traction from a 6x4 configuration.
  • Driver retention: Driver scarcity is a operational risk, and premium cab features are a competitive recruitment tool.
  • Brand positioning: Your fleet serves premium logistics contracts where the flagship image and lowest downtime are part of the value proposition.
  • Maintenance infrastructure: You have access to Eaton and Cummins Z14 service capabilities — the E1st's premium components require specialized support.

The Bottom Line

The Z3 and E1st are not competing products — they are complementary tools designed for different fleet strategies. The Z3 is the pragmatic choice: lower purchase price, proven M13 reliability, configuration flexibility, and the lowest per-kilometer operating cost in the SAGMOTO tractor lineup. It is the truck you buy when your routes are predictable, your drivers are experienced, and your spreadsheet demands the best cost-per-kilometer.

The E1st is the strategic choice: more power, predictive shifting, premium cab ergonomics, and the aerodynamic package to back it up. It costs more upfront and slightly more to fuel, but it compensates through reduced driver turnover, lower maintenance on grade-heavy routes, and the operational confidence that comes from having power in reserve when the load is heavy and the road goes uphill. For fleets running 49-ton GCW across mountainous terrain — the Central Asia corridor, the East African highlands, or the Andean routes of South America — the E1st's premium is an investment in uptime and driver retention that pays back over the truck's service life.
